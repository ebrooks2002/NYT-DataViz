A Data Visualization Project

Investigating Trends in The New York Times Through Visualization

By Ethan Brooks and Tamur Asar

Four views into a collection of over two million New York Times articles, tracing the cultural and semantic patterns that run through them — and what those patterns suggest about the wider news landscape.

Section Production

Which New York Times sections produce the most news?

Each tile is one section, sized by its share of everything published that year and shaded by article count.

Stepping through the years shows a newsroom reacting: New York swells after 9/11, Business Day during the 2007 financial crisis, World through the 2003 Iraq War. Some sections hold a steady share; others move sharply year to year.

Common Words

What were the most common words of each year?

The ten keywords that grew fastest against the previous year. Larger, darker words grew more.

The results track the news cycle closely: “New York City” and “Terrorism” in 2001, “Iraq” and “Housing” in 2003, “Barack Obama” and “United States Economy” in 2008.

Sentiment Trends

How do news events shift the tone of coverage across sections?

Sentiment analysis scored the tone of every article; the lines track what share of a section's coverage read positive, neutral, or negative.

Major events move every section at once — 2001 and 2008 both register sharp shifts — but sections differ in how far they swing.

Sentiment by Section

Which sections lean most negative? Which lean most positive?

Tone broken down within each section for a single year.

Between 2000 and 2010, World never falls below 50% negative — year after year, most world coverage concerns conflict. Travel runs the opposite way, emphasizing the beauty of the world. Whether that gap reflects the world itself or what readers seek is the open question.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Read together, these four views show a newsroom that moves with its moment — publishing volume shifting between sections, keywords spiking with the news cycle, and tone bending around the events of the year.

We hope they prompt further exploration of media trends. Our thanks to our professor, Bret Jackson, for teaching us the visualization tools and techniques this project required.
